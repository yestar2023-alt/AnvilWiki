## What map options does Early Access include?

The Steam page says the current Early Access version lets you play missions in multiple large cities created by the developer. It also lists Steam Workshop support for custom maps and an included level editor. The presskit describes the cities as real-world locations and names New York, Paris, and Los Angeles.

That means there are two broad ways to play: developer-made city scenarios and player-made maps distributed through Steam Workshop. The official material does not promise a fixed number of maps, a campaign order, or a complete modding API. Treat map availability as something to check on the store and Workshop pages at the time you play.

## How should you choose a Workshop map?

Read the map description before subscribing. A useful listing explains the intended challenge, the city or fictional setting, the approximate scale, and whether the map is meant for solo or co-op play. It should also state the game version it was built for. During Early Access, a map made for an older build may not behave as intended.

Look for signs that the creator has tested the scenario. Clear screenshots, a concise changelog, and specific notes about known issues are more useful than a dramatic title. Comments can reveal whether players encounter broken spawns, unreachable civilians, or severe performance problems, but they are player reports rather than official guarantees.

If you plan to play with a friend, check the [co-op guide](/co-op/) before choosing a map that may demand two separate responsibilities. For performance concerns, the [system requirements guide](/guides/system-requirements/) covers the official hardware baseline.

## What makes a custom map readable?

A readable map gives players enough information to form a plan. Major roads, bridges, water, and dense districts should be visually distinct. Civilian areas should not be hidden behind ambiguous scenery. Defensive positions should have clear approaches, and the map should make it obvious where a breach could spread.

Scale matters too. A very large city can feel impressive, but it may spread the player's attention and resources too thin. A smaller map with clear chokepoints can teach the core loop better.

## What should map creators document?

Creators should state the map's purpose, recommended player count, known limitations, and the build used for testing. A short design note explaining intended civilian pressure and defensive routes helps players understand why the map is arranged as it is. Avoid claiming balance that has not been tested across multiple runs.

The official page confirms Workshop uploading and playing, but it does not publish a detailed editor manual in the store description. Do not assume that every object or rule from the base game is exposed in the editor. Test the map from a clean start, including the opening preparation phase and at least one late-game surge.

## How do base-game skills transfer?

The core skills transfer: read the city, protect civilians, keep a fallback, and adapt when the perimeter fails. What changes is the amount of uncertainty. A custom map may use unfamiliar density, route structures, or spawn pressure, so a build that worked in a developer city may need a different shape.

Start custom maps with a conservative plan and observe before committing heavily. The [beginner guide](/beginner-guide/) explains the basic loop, while the [civilian protection guide](/guides/civilian-protection/) focuses on the objective that matters most. If a map feels unfair rather than difficult, check its version and description before assuming your strategy is the problem.
